Four faces of inequality

Wealth and income are treated as one story. They came apart decades ago, and which one you measure decides what you conclude.

    Wealth

    Wealth concentrates harder than income. The same country often has a wealth Gini twenty points above its income Gini, and the gap between the two has widened. This is the series the atlas is built on, harmonized from five sources with each row tagged for comparability.

    Why

    Since 1990 the richest one per cent of Americans went from holding 29% of everything owned in the country to holding 35%. Over the same years the bottom half went from 1.7% to 1.0%, and spent eleven of those years below zero: they collectively owed more than they owned. That is the transfer. It is a change in who owns the country, and it happened to wealth, not to pay.

    Who owns the United States

    Owning and earning are different things, and the gap between them is the argument. Take the same top one per cent, measured the same way by the same source, once in what it owns and once in what it earns. In 1990 it took 14.7% of American income and held 29.0% of American wealth. By 2021 it took 19.0% of income and held 36.3% of wealth. Both rose. Wealth started higher and rose faster, by 7.2 points against 4.3.

    The same top 1%

    Now the same country and the same years through a Gini, which is the number almost every report quotes. It barely moves. A Gini compresses the whole curve into one figure, and at 0.83 it has little room left: the top hundredth can take seven more points of everything and the Gini shrugs. The bottom half falling through zero is a rounding error inside it. This is why the atlas keeps shares, and why a single inequality number is close to useless for the question people actually mean.

    Not only America

    The bottom half's share of wealth fell in most rich countries over the same years: 13.1% to 4.6% in the United Kingdom, 10.2% to 2.5% in Italy, 8.9% to 4.8% in France. The Netherlands moved the other way.

    The middle, squeezed

    The American middle 40%, everyone between the median and the top tenth, took 44.4% of national income in 1990 and 40.6% in 2021. Ask the income face for the middle 40% to follow it anywhere else.

    Owning is not earning

    The bottom half of America earns 13.8% of national income and owns 0.8% of national wealth. Those are the same people, counted the same way, in the same year.

    The database

    Five source families in one long panel: WID, the ECB household survey (HFCS), the Luxembourg Wealth Study, the US Survey of Consumer Finances, and the Fed's Distributional Financial Accounts. 10,783 rows with a non-null Gini, ISO3 keys, 1800 to 2025 in the release files.

    Comparability flags

    Every row carries comparability_tier (A for harmonized surveys, B for mixed methodology) and top_tail_flag (survey only, or enhanced with administrative data).

    Filtering to like-for-like rows is one line of pandas.

    What the ask tab reads

    The WID Gini series (1990 to 2024, per-adult equal-split, negative wealth zeroed), latest distribution shares by country, and the US long-run series back to 1989.
